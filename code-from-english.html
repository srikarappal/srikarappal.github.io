What Is the Engineer For?

A 2018 proposal to turn English into code. What came true, what is still open and what the craft becomes.

I originally sent this email on July 9, 2018 to my then manager, as a project proposal. A lot has happened since. Trying to date it: the email is four weeks after GPT-1, the transformers paper was a year old, Copilot was still three years out, scaling laws four years off and ChatGPT almost five. I was trying to show my research on a model that goes from an English description of a task to low-level code.

I had a working version hacked together from a frankenstein of LSTMs and Transformers. I trained it on all of Amazon's internal code repos (which is quite a lot). I wrote that the deep learning we had then could not sufficiently do it. It could write smaller functions, but anything more complex and the model would just repeat. Mind you, this is about a 120M parameter model (it was a mess trying to stabilize training for a largish LSTM). Current methods and infrastructure were just not enough. I also wrote that if it ever worked, it would change the entire nature of the software industry and what engineers do.

What I actually wrote

The screenshot is not proof that I invented code generation. A lot of people could have said "code from English" in 2018. Seq2seq existed. The architecture papers were on the table if you were paying attention. What I actually wrote was narrower than that.

The first claim was a technical judgment from inside the work. GPT-1 had just landed as a language-understanding result. It was not going to write a working program from a paragraph. There was a gap between "this might scale" and "this emits software." I was sitting in that gap, training a model that could not yet cross it. Current methods were not enough. That was true.

The second claim is the one I couldn't shake. If description-to-code ever got cheap, engineering would not get a smarter autocomplete. It would get a new question: what is the engineer for? I was ready to quit my job to chase this full time, but visa troubles got in the way.

Why it should have worked

I remember having a very strong conviction that it should work. For the life of me I could not figure out why I couldn't get it to. Code, in any language, is a very structured prediction problem. It is almost a translation problem: English to Python, C, C++. At the time, translation was being swept up by transformers, so I extrapolated to code. I was sure it should be easy for a model, especially since a programming language has much less "vocabulary" and far more riding on structural correctness and syntactic sugar. Little did I know, I was missing one key ingredient: scaling, in both model size and data.

The second half is still open

The models got very good, very fast, at emitting code. We did not get correspondingly good at deciding which code, why this system, what to trust, what to throw away or how a team of humans should spend a day when typing is no longer the bottleneck. Things are about to get more intense, with thousands of agents spinning up competing versions of a system, all vying to be the one contender that gets picked. It is very possible the coding models get so scary good that no human needs to look behind the curtain at how the sausage is made. Any bug, ask the model to take care of it. (I wrote ThinkingSDK in that light.)

All written or spoken communication is lossy and discrete. It is open to interpretation, especially English. No amount of written direction is precise enough to capture every little design decision you want out of the coding agent writing your software. For the foreseeable future, humans will be an integral part of making software, guiding these models toward the design we actually want instead of whatever the model reaches for.

There was a brief time, in late 2025, when I felt like a fraud using coding agents. Mostly hubris. It is now an invaluable tool, ignore it at your own peril, especially if the rest of your team is using it. The craft of being a software engineer is changing, from writing lines of code to having a taste for good design: what fits here, what leads to fewer bugs and rewrites later. We are in a wild and transformative phase of the software industry.

Srikar Appalaraju
